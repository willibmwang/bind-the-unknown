In plain terms

The question. A new virus turns up. Which of its proteins should a drug aim at? Normally a person answers that, from the literature and from viruses already understood.

The input is one accession number, the public database ID for a genome, and nothing else. From it, five steps work out which proteins the virus makes, rank them as drug targets, predict one protein's 3-D shape — nobody has solved a structure for a brand-new virus, so a predicted one is all there is to measure — and check whether a drug would have anywhere on it to grip. No person names the target.

The test below ran on SARS-CoV-2 with SARS-CoV-2 itself hidden from every database it could reach, so it could not simply recognise the answer. It still put the real drug targets at the top, including the one an approved antiviral hits.

From a genome to a ranked shortlist

Five steps, run live against public databases. Out comes a ranked list of targets, every score broken into its parts, and every surface either measured on a deposited structure, computed on a predicted one, or refused with a reason.

In

The {{identified}} it ranked, and why each one scored what it did

Nothing is read off an existing annotation of this genome. The sequence is translated in all six reading frames, every stretch between stop codons of at least {{minimum_orf_aa}} residues is kept, and the {{orfs_ranked}} longest are searched against {{reference_entries}} reviewed viral proteins with SARS-CoV-2 deleted from them.

Shared five-residue runs settle a close relative in milliseconds and are what the match column reports; a slower pass groups residues by chemical type for fainter resemblances and reports a bit score instead. With no relative at either tier, a protein is classed by hydropathy, which returns a class rather than a name and cannot outrank a real match.

The spike and the replicase finish within a few hundredths of each other by opposite routes: the spike for sitting on the outside, the replicase for matching a known relative. Only that match is measured against this genome — the other four columns are read off whichever relative was matched, so holding the pathogen out lowers the scores instead of emptying the list.

In

Out

  • {{chains}} protein chains out of the {{identified}} identified ORFs — {{cut_chains}} cut out of a larger parent, {{whole_chains}} already whole

  • Scored on the same five weights as the ORFs above, {{score_high}} down to {{score_low}} of a possible {{score_ceiling}}. The {{biggest_cut}} chains from one ORF alone span {{cut_high}} to {{cut_low}}: the spread the parent's single score hides

The {{chains}} chains it produced, and what each one scored

A mature chain is what the virus actually folds, and what a binder is aimed at. A polyprotein is made as one long protein and cut into them by the virus's own proteases, at boundaries annotated on the curated relative each ORF matched, and every row links back to the ORF it came out of.

The weights are stage 1's. The match to a relative is inherited from the parent, so chains out of one ORF share one number, while the structure count and the two class terms are re-read per chain.

That is the one chain the record reports in full. Below is every chain this step was handed, with where its sequence was found in this genome and where it fell against the ceiling.

Boundaries are annotated on the relative, not here, and slicing this genome at them fails silently: SARS-CoV's main protease starts at residue 3241 and SARS-CoV-2's at 3264, so the same cut returns 306 residues — the right length, 23 residues into the wrong window. Each chain is aligned instead, against every called ORF rather than only the one it matched.

Being too long bars folding the chain whole, not reaching the target. What a drug is aimed at is almost never a whole chain — it is a pocket, an interface, a catalytic site — and the relative's entry already says where those are. So each over-the-ceiling row below opens onto the regions its relative annotates, and {{domain_rescued}} of {{domain_over}} have at least one short enough to fold. {{domain_richest_chain}} is the clearest case: {{domain_richest_whole}} residues whole, with {{domain_richest_count}} regions inside it that are not — the first offered is {{domain_richest_best}}, at {{domain_richest_best_aa}} residues.

Which region a row offers first comes down to two things, and the panel says which did the work. First, how many residues the curators marked as active, ligand-binding or cleavage sites fall inside it — decisive for enzymes: the polymerase's catalytic domain holds six against two for the domain beside it. Second, failing that, a list of words we chose (receptor-binding, catalytic, interface and about twenty more) matched against the description.

That list orders rows and nothing else: it refuses no region and touches no rank. Regions under {{domain_minimum_aa}} residues are not folded at all, since coordinates for a five-residue fragment describe nothing.

What has already been written about each of these

A region with coordinates is one a binder could be designed against this week, so this is the point in the run where asking the literature becomes worth doing. Every shortlisted target is searched, and so is each domain the step above folded, through Europe PMC — chosen over PubMed because at onset the preprints are the literature, and PubMed indexes none of them.

The difficulty is that a domain's curated description is not a search term. Macro 1 retrieves software engineering; Y1 retrieves almost anything. So each description is anchored to the protein it belongs to before it is sent, and the query that was actually sent is printed on every row below — a count whose question is not recorded cannot be checked, and a hit count is evidence of attention and of nothing else. It says a field is crowded. It says nothing about whether a protein is essential, exposed, or worth a binder.

4Measure the surface a drug would grip{{surface_seconds}} s

In

  • {{chains}} chains
  • And the coordinates step 3 predicted, which is the only thing there is to measure when nothing has been solved

Out

  • {{rows_out}} answers for {{chains}} chains, {{surface_dropped}} quietly dropped
  • {{measured}} measured on real deposited structures, {{unmeasured}} refused with a stated reason
  • {{surface_predicted}} of those {{unmeasured}} are inside the folding ceiling, so step 3's coordinates are the only surface they can have — measured with the same arithmetic, never added to the deposited numbers
  • {{surface_any}} of {{chains}} chains have a surface by one route or the other, against {{measured}} from depositions alone; {{surface_none}} have neither
  • Every patch is measured against the {{footprint}} Å² a macrocycle buries, the line the lane below draws through every chain

What the measurement returned, chain by chain

One row for every chain, each answering a single question: if you wanted to put a molecule on this protein, is there anywhere on it to put one?

A patch is water-repelling surface, the part a molecule of this class grips, in square angstroms. The line through every row is the roughly {{footprint}} Å² a macrocycle covers when it binds, and the fainter line is half of that, under which there is nothing to hold a molecule at all. Solved and predicted shapes share the scale.

A measured chain gets a dot and a bar, because a structure is one moment of a protein held still and the same chain solved twice does not give the same answer. The dot is the middle of every structure of that domain, the bar runs from smallest to largest, and where a bar straddles the line the verdict is uncertain however confident the dot looks. Nothing drops out quietly: a refused chain still gets a row saying why.

Why the predicted group is kept apart, when the sum is the same arithmetic. A deposited structure is something that happened: a protein was purified, crystallised or frozen, put in a beam, and coordinates were fitted to what came back. A predicted structure is a claim a model makes from the sequence, with no one having looked at the molecule.

The deposited patch comes with a spread, because the same chain has been solved several times and the structures disagree. A prediction has no such bar — the same sequence returns the same coordinates — only a confidence per residue, {{mean_plddt}} out of 100 for the main protease.

A predicted chain is also folded alone, with no partner and no membrane around it, so faces that are buried in life sit out in the open and the patch inflates. That is the likeliest reason the main protease's predicted patch comes out several times larger than its deposited one, and it is not separable from ordinary prediction error — so the record states the ratio and names no cause.

The predicted group earns its place anyway. A deposited structure exists only where somebody already did the work, which tracks attention rather than importance: the two enzymes approved antivirals hit have several structures apiece, while the membrane and accessory proteins have none anywhere.

A pipeline that can only measure depositions answers best on the targets that need it least, and goes quiet on a genuinely new pathogen — the case this project exists for. The prediction does not replace a measurement; it turns no answer at all into an answer that says where it came from.

That gate is a statement about this class of molecule and not about the target, so it never becomes a term in the ranking.

The shortlist

{{identified}} identified ORFs cut into {{chains}} chains

This is the deliverable, and a different list from step 1's rather than a shorter version of it: step 1 ranks whole stretches of genome, step 2 cuts them into the mature chains a binder is actually aimed at. Only the {{identified}} ORFs that matched a relative can be cut, since the boundaries come from that relative, so the {{unidentified}} unrecognised ones end here. Those {{identified}} become {{chains}} chains, and {{biggest_cut}} of them come out of a single ORF: the replicase, step 1's second row.

The two chains that approved antivirals already hit are not ORFs at all. The main protease (nsp5), which nirmatrelvir inhibits, is rank {{mpro_rank}} here; the polymerase (nsp12), which remdesivir inhibits, is rank {{rdrp_rank}}. Both are interior fragments of that one replicase row, so a pipeline that stopped at step 1 could not have surfaced either. With SARS-CoV-2 deleted from the reference library, both still land in the top five — scored only against relatives, never against the virus itself.
