One pathogen genome in, every next step out

When a new virus appears, someone has to decide which of its proteins a drug should aim at. The evidence for that decision is thinnest when the virus is newest. This system makes the decision from the genome's database ID alone, and records how it got there at every step.

In plain terms

A new virus turns up. Which of its proteins should a drug aim at? A person usually answers that question, from the literature and from viruses already understood.

What goes in

One accession number, the public database ID for a genome. Nothing else: no target name, no shortlist, no structure.

What runs

Five steps work out which proteins the virus makes, rank them as drug targets, predict one protein's 3-D shape, and check whether a drug would have anywhere on it to grip. No person names the target at any point.

Nobody has solved a structure for a brand-new virus, so a predicted one is all there is to measure.

What comes out

A ranked shortlist of the proteins a drug could be aimed at. Each score is broken into its parts, and each surface is measured, predicted, or refused with a reason.

The test below ran on SARS-CoV-2, with SARS-CoV-2 itself hidden from every database the system could reach, so it could not recognise the answer. It still put the real drug targets at the top, including the one an approved antiviral hits.

From a genome to a ranked shortlist

Five steps run live against public databases. They return a ranked list of targets, each score broken into its parts, and each surface either measured on a deposited structure, computed on a predicted one, or refused with a reason.

The {{identified}} it ranked, and why each one scored what it did

Nothing is read off an existing annotation of this genome. The sequence is translated in all six reading frames. Every stretch between stop codons of at least {{minimum_orf_aa}} residues is kept, and the {{orfs_ranked}} longest are searched against {{reference_entries}} reviewed viral proteins, with SARS-CoV-2 deleted from them.

How a relative is matched, and what happens when there is nonetwo tiers, then a fallback

A close relative is found from shared five-residue runs, which takes milliseconds and is what the match column reports. A slower pass groups residues by chemical type to catch fainter resemblances, and reports a bit score instead.

A protein that reaches no relative at either tier is classed by how water-repelling its residues are. That returns a class rather than a name, and cannot outrank a real match.

The spike and the replicase finish within a few hundredths of each other, by opposite routes: the spike scores for sitting on the outside of the virus, the replicase for matching a known relative. Only the match is measured against this genome. The other four columns are read off whichever relative was matched, which is why holding the pathogen out lowers the scores rather than emptying the list.

The {{chains}} chains it produced, and what each one scored

A virus often makes one long protein and then cuts it into shorter chains with its own enzymes. Those chains are what it folds, and what a drug is aimed at. The cut points are annotated on the curated relative each ORF matched, and every row links back to the ORF it came out of.

The weights are the five used in step 1. The match to a relative is inherited from the parent, so chains cut from one ORF share that number, while the structure count and the two class terms are read again for each chain.

What the measurement returned, chain by chain

One row for every chain, each answering one question: is there anywhere on this protein for a molecule to bind? A patch is a continuous stretch of water-repelling surface, measured in square angstroms, and it is the part a molecule of this class grips. A refused chain still gets a row, and the row says why it was refused.

How to read a row
the chain how its coordinates were come by, and why it was refused if it was 858 Å²758–957
  • how much grip there is. Every bar starts at zero and they share one scale, solved and predicted together.
  • the {{footprint}} Å² a macrocycle covers. Past the line there is room for one. Under half its length there is nothing for a molecule to hold.
  • how far repeat solutions disagree. The bar is the middle answer. Where this crosses the line, the measurement does not settle the question.
  • measured on a predicted shape. Outlined, never filled, and never added to the deposited count.
Why the predicted group is kept apart, when the sum is the same arithmetic4 notes

A deposited structure is something that happened: a protein was purified, crystallised or frozen, put in a beam, and coordinates were fitted to what came back. A predicted structure is a claim a model makes from the sequence, with no one having looked at the molecule.

The deposited patch comes with a spread, because the same chain has been solved several times and the structures disagree. A prediction has no such bar — the same sequence returns the same coordinates — only a confidence per residue, {{mean_plddt}} out of 100 for the main protease.

A predicted chain is also folded alone, with no partner and no membrane around it, so faces that are buried in life sit out in the open and the patch inflates. That is the likeliest reason the main protease's predicted patch comes out several times larger than its deposited one. It cannot be separated from ordinary prediction error, so the record states the ratio and names no cause.

That gate is a statement about this class of molecule and not about the target, so it never becomes a term in the ranking.

Why a predicted surface earns its place at all2 notes

A deposited structure exists only where somebody already did the work, which tracks attention rather than importance: the two enzymes approved antivirals hit have several structures apiece, while the membrane and accessory proteins have none anywhere.

A pipeline that can only measure deposited structures answers best on the targets that need it least, and says nothing at all about a genuinely new pathogen, which is the case this project exists for. A prediction does not replace a measurement. It replaces no answer with an answer that states where it came from.

Is the patch where the chemistry is?

Everything above measures the largest water-repelling patch anywhere on a structure. That answers whether the protein could hold a molecule of this class at all. The next question is where on the protein to put one. The curators have already marked the residues that do the work, the catalytic and ligand-binding ones, so the same arithmetic can be run at those residues instead of at the chain's best patch.

1 · mark Take the residues the curated entry marks as doing the work.
2 · reach Collect everything within {{site_reach}} Å of them. A macrocycle covers about {{footprint}} Å² when it binds, which makes it roughly that far across.
3 · intersect Keep the part that is also in the patch step 4 found. {{site_measured}} of {{site_units}} folded units have at least one marked residue inside the coordinates. The rest state which of four absences applies.

Step 4's question has a yes-or-no answer: does the unit's best patch reach the marked residues at all? {{site_patch_at_site}} of {{site_measured}} units say yes, which is too coarse to act on. The share of the patch that sits at those residues says how much. {{site_high_unit}} holds {{site_high_share}} % of its unit's patch there, and {{site_low_unit}} holds {{site_low_share}} %, so most of the surface that second unit was scored on is somewhere else. Both count as a yes.

Not every marked residue is a place to put a binderwhy two figures are reported

On a coronavirus polyprotein, most plain site annotations mark the points where an enzyme cuts the chain. A cut point sits at the end of a finished chain by construction, so a neighbourhood drawn around one is a neighbourhood of a loose end. Both measurements are therefore reported.

{{site_drop_unit}} is where it costs the most: {{site_drop_full}} Å² of its {{site_drop_whole}} Å² patch at all its marked residues, {{site_drop_full_share}} % — and {{site_drop_chem}} Å², {{site_drop_chem_share}} %, over the residues that do chemistry once its {{site_drop_dropped}} cleavage site{{site_drop_plural}} are dropped.

Read the confidence firstthese are predicted coordinates

{{site_anchors_low}} of the {{site_anchors}} marked residues sit below the folding tool's own confidence threshold. Many of them are cut points, and are disordered because they are chain ends rather than because of anything about the chemistry.

{{site_processing_only}} units have nothing but cleavage boundaries in them, and they are grouped apart below.

How a curated position was carried into these coordinatesand what is withheld if it did not land

A marked position is curated on the relative's entry, carried through the alignment into one reading frame of this genome, then into coordinates numbered from one. Each row was checked against the residue the relative's entry actually carries at that position, and {{site_at_annotated}} of {{site_measured}} land on it.

A row that did not land on it would have its figures withheld, because a patch measured at the wrong residues looks exactly like one measured at the right ones.

The rows run along {{site_frames}} different reading frames. {{site_overlaps}} pairs of them carry spans that overlap as numbers while sharing no residue, so every row names its frame.

The shortlist

{{identified}} identified ORFs cut into {{chains}} chains

This is the output. It is a different list from step 1's, not a shorter version of it: step 1 ranks whole stretches of genome, and step 2 cuts them into the chains a drug is aimed at. Only the {{identified}} ORFs that matched a relative can be cut, since the cut points come from that relative, so the {{unidentified}} unrecognised ones end here. Those {{identified}} become {{chains}} chains, {{biggest_cut}} of which come out of a single ORF: the replicase, step 1's second row.

The two chains that approved antivirals already hit are not ORFs at all. The main protease (nsp5), which nirmatrelvir inhibits, is rank {{mpro_rank}} here; the polymerase (nsp12), which remdesivir inhibits, is rank {{rdrp_rank}}. Both sit inside that one replicase row, so a pipeline that stopped at step 1 could not have found either. With SARS-CoV-2 deleted from the reference library, both still land in the top five, scored only against relatives and never against the virus itself.
