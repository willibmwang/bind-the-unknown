In plain terms

The question. A new virus turns up. Which of its proteins should a drug aim at? Normally a person answers that, from the literature and from viruses already understood.

The input is one accession number, the public database ID for a genome, and nothing else. From it, five steps work out which proteins the virus makes, rank them as drug targets, predict one protein's 3-D shape — nobody has solved a structure for a brand-new virus, so a predicted one is all there is to measure — and check whether a drug would have anywhere on it to grip. No person names the target.

The test below ran on SARS-CoV-2 with SARS-CoV-2 itself hidden from every database it could reach, so it could not simply recognise the answer. It still put the real drug targets at the top, including the one an approved antiviral hits.

Nothing is read off an existing annotation of this genome. The sequence is translated in all six reading frames, every stretch between stop codons of at least 60 residues is kept, and the 12 longest are searched against 17,512 reviewed viral proteins with SARS-CoV-2 deleted from them.

Shared five-residue runs settle a close relative in milliseconds and are what the match column reports; a slower pass groups residues by chemical type for fainter resemblances and reports a bit score instead. With no relative at either tier, a protein is classed by hydropathy, which returns a class rather than a name and cannot outrank a real match.

That is the one chain the record reports in full. Below is every chain this step was handed, with where its sequence was found in this genome and where it fell against the ceiling.

Boundaries are annotated on the relative, not here, and slicing this genome at them fails silently: SARS-CoV's main protease starts at residue 3241 and SARS-CoV-2's at 3264, so the same cut returns 306 residues — the right length, 23 residues into the wrong window. Each chain is aligned instead, against every called ORF rather than only the one it matched.

Being too long bars folding the chain whole, not reaching the target. What a drug is aimed at is almost never a whole chain — it is a pocket, an interface, a catalytic site — and the relative's entry already says where those are. So each over-the-ceiling row below opens onto the regions its relative annotates, and 9 of the 9 have at least one short enough to fold. Papain-like protease nsp3 is the clearest case: 1,945 residues whole, with 18 regions inside it that are not — the first offered is CoV Nsp3 Y, at 369 residues.

Which region a row offers first comes down to two things, and the panel says which did the work. First, how many residues the curators marked as active, ligand-binding or cleavage sites fall inside it — decisive for enzymes: the polymerase's catalytic domain holds six against two for the domain beside it. Second, failing that, a list of words we chose (receptor-binding, catalytic, interface and about twenty more) matched against the description.

That list orders rows and nothing else: it refuses no region and touches no rank. Regions under 40 residues are not folded at all, since coordinates for a five-residue fragment describe nothing.

Several of their patches clear the footprint line comfortably and are refused anyway. That is the gate saying something about this class of molecule, not about the target.

Why the predicted group is kept apart, when the sum is the same arithmetic. A deposited structure is something that happened: a protein was purified, crystallised or frozen, put in a beam, and coordinates were fitted to what came back. A predicted structure is a claim a model makes from the sequence, with no one having looked at the molecule.

The deposited patch comes with a spread, because the same chain has been solved several times and the structures disagree. A prediction has no such bar — the same sequence returns the same coordinates — only a confidence per residue, 62.6 out of 100 for the main protease.

A predicted chain is also folded alone, with no partner and no membrane around it, so faces that are buried in life sit out in the open and the patch inflates. That is the likeliest reason the main protease's predicted patch comes out several times larger than its deposited one, and it is not separable from ordinary prediction error — so the record states the ratio and names no cause.

The predicted group earns its place anyway. A deposited structure exists only where somebody already did the work, which tracks attention rather than importance: the two enzymes approved antivirals hit have several structures apiece, while the membrane and accessory proteins have none anywhere.

A pipeline that can only measure depositions answers best on the targets that need it least, and goes quiet on a genuinely new pathogen — the case this project exists for. The prediction does not replace a measurement; it turns no answer at all into an answer that says where it came from.

That gate is a statement about this class of molecule and not about the target, so it never becomes a term in the ranking.
