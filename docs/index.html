One pathogen genome in, every next step out

When a new virus appears, someone has to decide which of its proteins a drug should aim at. That is expert judgement, and the evidence it rests on is thinnest exactly when the pathogen is new. This works it out from the accession alone, and shows what it did at every step — so an expert can check it rather than take its word.

In plain terms

The question. A new virus turns up. Which of its proteins should a drug aim at? Picking that target is normally expert judgement — reading the literature, and comparing the new virus to ones that are already understood.

What this does. It takes one accession number — the public database ID for a genome — and nothing else. From that it works out which proteins the virus makes, ranks them by how promising a drug target each one looks, predicts one protein's 3-D shape, and checks whether a drug would have anywhere on it to grip. Shape first, because for a virus nobody has studied there is no solved structure to look at, and a predicted one is the only thing left to measure. Five steps, about a minute and a half, with no person naming the target.

The test below. It was given SARS-CoV-2 — with SARS-CoV-2 itself removed from everything it was allowed to look up, so it could not simply recognise the answer. It still put the real drug targets at the top, including the one an approved antiviral already hits.

One nucleotide accession is the whole input. No target name, no shortlist, no structure. The same five steps you just read run against the same live databases, and the result becomes a page like this one.

Open the request form

For a genome split across segments, separate the accessions with commas. A genome too large to be viral is refused rather than ranked: this is antiviral work, and the reference it identifies against is reviewed viral proteins.

From a genome to a ranked shortlist

Five steps, run live against public databases. Everything below this line is the path to the deliverable: a list of candidate targets, ranked, with every score decomposed and every surface either measured on a deposited structure, computed on a predicted one, or refused with a reason.

The 12 it ranked, and why each one scored what it did

Those counts are what this step reports. Below is what it actually returned. Nothing is fetched from an existing annotation of this genome: the sequence is translated in all six reading frames, every stretch between stop codons of at least 60 residues is kept — overlaps in different frames included, because discarding one of two overlapping frames here would let length decide what homology should — and the 12 longest are searched against 17,512 reviewed viral proteins with SARS-CoV-2 itself deleted from that reference. Identification is tried in three tiers, cheapest first: exact 5-mer identity, then reduced-alphabet seeding with Smith–Waterman where that finds nothing, then hydropathy, which returns a class rather than a name and is scored so it cannot outrank a real homology hit.

The 20 chains it produced, and what each one scored

A mature chain is what the virus actually folds, and what a binder would actually be aimed at: a polyprotein above is cut into pieces by the virus's own proteases, and the boundaries used here are the ones annotated on the curated relative each ORF matched. Every row below names the ORF it came out of and links back to that row in the table above, so the cut is traceable in both directions. The five weights are stage 1's, unchanged — what moves is which of them are re-read for each piece. Conservation is inherited from the parent, measured once over the whole stretch, which is why chains out of one ORF carry one number between them. The structure count is recounted against each chain's own description; the two class terms are re-read from that description, with a chain that is most of its parent allowed to keep the parent's and a small cleavage product not; and where the curated entry annotates compartments per molecule rather than per entry, exposure is resolved for the chain instead of inherited. Those are the terms that separate the pieces of one polyprotein from each other.

The 12 chains taken out of the replicase are the clearest case: they span 6.90 to 2.50, and what separates the top of that spread from the bottom is almost entirely whether the chain's own description names a class drugs have historically been found against. The main protease and the polymerase sit at the top; most of their neighbours in the same polyprotein sit at the bottom, earning the inherited conservation term and little else — which is also the honest reading of this stage, since a chain nobody has described in those words is not thereby a bad target. Read the other direction, the three spike chains are identical in four of the five columns and separated by nothing but the count of structures already deposited across their relatives: hundredths of a point, a difference this ranking should not be read as resolving.

The shortlist

9 identified ORFs cut into 20 chains

This is the deliverable, and it is a different list from the one in step 1 — not a shorter version of it. Step 1 ranks whole stretches of genome; step 2 cuts the polyproteins among them into the mature chains a virus actually folds, and a binder is aimed at a chain. Only the 9 ORFs that matched a relative can be cut, since the boundaries come from that relative — the 3 unidentified ones end here. Those 9 become 20 chains, and 12 of them come out of a single ORF — the replicase, step 1's second row. Every chain below names the ORF it was cut from and links back to it. This is the ranking those scores produce; step 2 above carries the same 20 chains with each score split into the five weights that paid it.

Why the cut is what makes this section worth reading: the two chains that carry the claim do not exist at the ORF altitude at all. With SARS-CoV-2 deleted from the reference library, both still land in the top five — scored only against relatives, never against the virus itself.
